# Shortcuts

Ways to make the same material land faster and stay longer. Each one rests on a well-replicated finding about how memory and understanding work. Choose one or two per explanation, for this concept and this learner (`LEARNER.md` and `NOTES.md` say what has landed before), and log the choice in the lesson or challenge file. A shortcut speeds up encoding; it never replaces retrieval, spacing or the explain-back.

## Skeleton first

- **When:** the first lesson or knowledge block of a new area.
- **How:** before any detail, give the whole map in five to seven boxes and how they connect: what goes in, what comes out, the one loop or chain that does the work. Every later detail gets hung on a named box.
- **Why it works:** an advance organiser gives new facts a schema to attach to (Ausubel; Mayer's pre-training principle).
- **Pitfall:** a skeleton with more than seven parts is a syllabus, not a skeleton.

## Analogy bridge

- **When:** the learner knows a domain with the same structure (for a software engineer: queues, caches, call stacks, retries, abstraction leaks).
- **How:** map the parts one to one ("voltage is like…, current is like…"), then say exactly **where the analogy breaks**. Ask the learner to find one more correspondence themselves.
- **Why it works:** structure mapping lets the learner reuse a whole existing schema instead of building one (Gentner; Richland & Simms 2015).
- **Pitfall:** an analogy left unbroken becomes a misconception. The break is part of the lesson.

## Predict first

- **When:** the start of every lesson; before revealing any result that can be guessed.
- **How:** ask one question the learner can only guess at yet ("what happens to the current if…?"). Say that guesses aren't graded. Teach, then come back to the guess.
- **Why it works:** pretesting and generation improve later recall even when the guess is wrong, provided feedback follows (Richland, Kornell & Kao 2009; Kornell, Hays & Bjork 2009).
- **Pitfall:** grading the guess. It is a hook, not a check.

## Worked example → faded example → problem

- **When:** any procedure or problem type the learner hasn't solved before.
- **How:** first show an expert solving one, narrating why at each step, and ask "why this step?" about one of them. Then give a similar problem with the last steps blank. Only then a full problem.
- **Why it works:** novices learn more from studying solutions than from solving (worked-example effect, Sweller & Cooper 1985); fading hands over control gradually (Renkl). Once the learner is competent, examples stop helping and problems take over (expertise reversal, Kalyuga 2003), so move on fast.
- **Pitfall:** opening a new area with a problem the learner has no schema for. It reads as a trick.

## Misconception first

- **When:** a concept has a well-known wrong model (current gets "used up"; `git pull` is "just download").
- **How:** state the wrong model plainly, show the one observation it can't explain, then give the right model.
- **Why it works:** refutation beats plain exposition at changing a held belief (refutation-text research, e.g. Tippett 2010).
- **Pitfall:** spending longer on the wrong model than the right one.

## Contrast pairs

- **When:** two concepts are easy to confuse (series vs parallel, `let` vs `const`, affect vs effect).
- **How:** teach them side by side with the one feature that tells them apart; mix examples of both and ask which is which.
- **Why it works:** interleaved, contrasting examples train discrimination, the skill of knowing which tool a problem needs (Kornell & Bjork 2008; Rohrer & Taylor 2007).
- **Pitfall:** contrasting before either concept is known at all. Teach each once, then contrast.

## Picture plus words

- **When:** anything with structure in space or time: circuits, architectures, flows, state machines.
- **How:** one diagram, labelled with the glossary terms, explained in words beside it. Nothing decorative.
- **Why it works:** words and images are encoded in two channels that reinforce each other (Paivio's dual coding; Mayer's multimedia and coherence principles).
- **Pitfall:** a diagram that needs its own legend. Simplify until it doesn't.

## Concrete, then abstract

- **When:** a formula, a rule or a pattern.
- **How:** one specific case with real numbers first, then the general form, then a second case the learner solves.
- **Why it works:** concreteness fading gets the grounding of concrete examples and the transfer of abstract ones (Fyfe et al. 2014).
- **Pitfall:** stopping at the concrete. The general form is what transfers.

## Small chunks

- **When:** always.
- **How:** at most about four new elements per explanation. If there are more, split the lesson.
- **Why it works:** working memory holds roughly four chunks at once (Cowan 2001); overload blocks encoding.
- **Pitfall:** counting "steps" instead of new ideas. Familiar steps cost little.

## Expert heuristics

- **When:** as soon as an area opens, not saved for the end.
- **How:** give the one or two rules of thumb practitioners actually use ("check the cheapest suspect first", "measure before you cut") and the reason each one works.
- **Why it works:** experts organise knowledge around deep principles, not surface features (Chi, Feltovich & Glaser 1981); a named heuristic hands the learner that organisation early.
- **Pitfall:** folklore. Cite the source, and say when a heuristic is a convention rather than evidence.

## Explain why

- **When:** after a fact or step lands, and in every third check.
- **How:** ask "why does that work?" or "why this step and not the other?". The learner answers in their own words.
- **Why it works:** elaborative interrogation and self-explanation tie the new fact to what is already known (Chi 1989; Dunlosky et al. 2013 rate both moderate utility).
- **Pitfall:** accepting a restatement as an explanation.

## Memory or field card

- **When:** deciding what the learner should carry in their head.
- **How:** memorise what is needed **fast**, **often**, or **to notice that something is wrong**. Put what is rare, exact or long (tables, flags, typical values, part numbers) on a field card and teach only where to find it and when to reach for it.
- **Why it works:** offloading to a reliable external aid frees capacity for what has to be fluent (cognitive offloading, Risko & Gilbert 2016).
- **Pitfall:** offloading something the learner needs in order to recognise the problem. You can't look up what you don't know to look for.

## Not shortcuts

These feel efficient and aren't. Don't use them; if the learner asks for them, say why briefly.

- **Re-reading and highlighting.** Low utility; they build fluency, not memory (Dunlosky et al. 2013).
- **Teaching to a "learning style".** Matching instruction to a preferred style (visual, auditory…) doesn't improve learning (Pashler et al. 2008). Match the format to the content instead.
- **Massed practice.** Ten in a row of the same problem feels strong today and fades by next week. Space and mix.
